# Orcish Grammar

The grammatical rules below are inferred solely from attested canon phrases. Orcish is an under-documented constructed language with very few surviving examples, so all grammatical claims are tentative.

## Morphology

### Compounding

Orcish relies heavily on compounding. Every multi-morpheme attested word is a compound of two shorter elements:

| Compound | Element 1 | Element 2 | Inferred Meaning |
|----------|-----------|-----------|------------------|
| Lok'tar | Lok (victory) | tar (uncertain) | "Victory" |
| Lok'tar ogar | Lok'tar (victory) | ogar (death) | "Victory or death" |
| Mak'gora | Mak (duel/combat) | gora (honor) | "Duel of honor" |
| Aka'Magosh | Aka (blessing) | Magosh (upon you/ancestors) | "Blessing upon you" |
| Kosh'harg | Kosh (gathering) | Harg (festival) | "Gathering-festival" |
| Zug zug | Zug (work) | Zug (work) | "Work work" → agreement |

The compounding pattern is consistently **head-initial**: the primary semantic element precedes the modifying or extending element.

### Reduplication

*Zug zug* demonstrates full reduplication. The duplicated form shifts meaning from the literal ("work work") to a pragmatic function ("agreement / yes / okay"). It is unclear whether reduplication is a productive grammatical process or limited to this single attested example.

### Morpheme Boundary Marking

Two orthographic markers separate morphemes:

1. **Apostrophe (')** — the most common boundary marker: *Lok'tar*, *Mak'gora*, *Aka'Magosh*, *Kosh'harg*, *Gul'dan*, *Lok'osh*
2. **Hyphen (-)** — appears in *Throm-ka* and *Nok-Karosh*

The functional difference between apostrophe and hyphen boundaries, if any, cannot be determined from available data. It may be purely orthographic.

### The *Lok'tar ogar* Problem

The phrase *Lok'tar ogar* ("Victory or death") presents an analytical difficulty:

- **Analysis A:** *Lok'* = victory, *-tar* = or, *ogar* = death → "Victory-or death"
- **Analysis B:** *Lok'tar* = victory (unanalyzable), *ogar* = "or death" → "Victory or-death"
- **Analysis C:** *Lok'tar* = victory, *ogar* = death, with an unexpressed conjunction → "Victory [or] death"

Analysis A is the most common fan analysis but requires *-tar* to function as a conjunction, which finds no other attestation. Analysis B requires *ogar* to be a compound of an unknown conjunction + "death." Analysis C assumes zero-marking for disjunction. None is confirmable with available data.

**Additional *Lok'* compounds from expanded sources:**

Two further compounds with the *Lok'* root are attested in novels and expanded lore:

- **Lok'narosh** — possibly "victory and honor" or a variant battle cry. If *-narosh* contains the *-osh* element discussed below, this phrase may mean "victorious warrior" or "one of victory," parallel to *Lok'osh*.
- **Lok'amon** — possibly "victory song" or "song of victory." The element *-amon* is otherwise unattested; if *-mon* relates to *-magosh* (from *Aka'Magosh*), it could be a suffix meaning "upon you" or "through ancestors," but this is [speculative].

Both phrases reinforce the productivity of the *Lok'* root in Orcish martial vocabulary.

### The *-osh* Suffix in Names

Seven orcish words and names share a final /ʃ/ (sh) element:

| Name | Final Sequence | Vowel Before /ʃ/ | Known Root? |
|------|---------------|-------------------|-------------|
| Garrosh | -osh /oʃ/ | /ɔ/ | No (Garr- unknown) |
| Lok'osh | -osh /oʃ/ | /ɔ/ | Yes (Lok' = victory) |
| Magosh | -osh /oʃ/ | /ɔ/ | Ambiguous |
| Lok'narosh | -osh /oʃ/ | /ɔ/ | Partially (Lok' = victory) |
| Nok-Karosh | -osh /oʃ/ | /ɔ/ | No |
| Kosh | -osh /oʃ/ | /ɔ/ | Yes (standalone: "gathering") |
| Grommash | -ash /ɑʃ/ | /ɑ/ | Yes (Grom = bravery) |

Six of seven words share the identical final sequence /oʃ/ — not merely "words ending in /ʃ/" but specifically /oʃ/. The only outlier is *Grommash* with /ɑʃ/.

#### Possible Analyses

**Analysis 1: *-osh* as a name-forming suffix meaning "son of" or "scion of"**

Both *Grommash* and *Garrosh* are sons of prominent orcish figures (Grommash = Grom's son; Garrosh = also Grom's son, though the "Gar-" element is unexplained). If *-osh* = "son/scion of," then:
- *Grommash* = *Gromm* + *-ash* (not *-osh*) — contradicts this analysis unless *-ash* and *-osh* are allomorphs
- *Garrosh* = *Garr* + *-osh* — plausible if *Garr-* is a personal name element
- *Lok'osh* = *Lok'* + *-osh* — "Scion of Victory"? Plausible semantically

The problem is that *Grommash* ends in *-ash*, not *-osh*. If *-ash* and *-osh* are allomorphs, the conditioning environment is unclear (both follow /r/ in a doubled consonant: *Grommash* /m:/, *Garrosh* /r:/).

**Analysis 2: *-osh* as a valor/warrior suffix**

If *Lok'* = "victory" (as inferred from *Lok'tar*), then *Lok'osh* could mean "victorious warrior" or "one of victory." Extending this:
- *-osh* could be a suffix meaning "warrior," "champion," or "one who embodies [root]"
- *Garrosh* = *Garr-* + *-osh* = "warrior of [Garr-]" — the *Garr-* root is unknown
- *Grommash* would need *-ash* as a variant, which weakens the analysis

**Analysis 3: *-osh* as a coincidental phonological pattern**

The simplest explanation: *-osh* is not a morpheme at all. The three names share a final /ʃ/ sound, but:
- *Grommash* ends in /ɑʃ/, *Garrosh* in /oʃ/, *Lok'osh* in /oʃ/ — the vowel quality differs
- /ʃ/ is a common coda in orcish names (*Grommash*, *Garrosh*, *Kosh'harg* → *harg* ends in /g/, but the /ʃ/ coda is still notable)
- Proper names in many languages share phonological patterns without shared morphology (cf. English *Richard*, *Edward*, *Howard* all ending in /d/ — this does not make /-d/ a suffix)

**Analysis 4: *-osh* = "gathering/assembly/one who gathers" (connected to *Kosh*)**

A key datum: **Kosh** /kɔʃ/ ("gathering") is a standalone word that *is* the sequence -osh. If *-osh* relates to *Kosh* ("gathering"), then:
- *Lok'osh* = "Victory-gathering" / "Assembly of victory" → a Spirit Walker title, one who gathers victory → "Victorious one" — plausible
- *Lok'narosh* = *Lok'* (victory) + *-nar-* (unknown) + *-osh* (gathering) → "Victory-[nar]-gathering" — variant battle cry confirming *Lok'* + *-osh* pattern
- *Kosh'harg* = *Kosh* (gathering) + *harg* (festival) = "Gathering-festival" — already the accepted analysis, consistent
- *Garrosh* = *Garr-* + *-osh* = "[Garr-]-gathering" / "one who gathers [Garr-]" — plausible
- *Magosh* may not decompose as *Mag- + -osh*; *Aka'Magosh* could have *Magosh* as an indivisible morpheme "upon you / ancestors"

The *Kosh* connection provides a semantic anchor for *-osh* = "gathering/assembly," but the conflict with *Magosh* (if *-osh* is a suffix, it conflicts with *Magosh*'s "upon you" meaning) remains an obstacle.

#### Assessment

**Confidence: Low–Moderate** for *-osh* as a genuine morphological suffix (upgraded from Very Low–Low). The upgrade is driven by: (1) six words ending in /oʃ/, not three — the pattern is more robust than previously assessed; (2) the *Kosh* connection provides a potential semantic anchor; (3) *Lok'osh* and *Lok'narosh* confirm the *Lok'* + *-osh* pattern is productive with an intervening element. Remaining obstacles: *Grommash*'s /ɑʃ/ vs. the consistent /oʃ/ elsewhere, and the *Magosh* conflict.

### The *Durotan / Durotar* Alternation: Personal Name vs. Place Name Suffixes

The pair *Durotan* (personal name) and *Durotar* (place name) differs only in the final consonant: **/n/ ↔ /r/**.

| Name | Ending | Category | IPA |
|------|--------|----------|-----|
| Durotan | -tan /tɑn/ | Personal name | /ˈdʊrɔtɑn/ |
| Durotar | -tar /tɑr/ | Place name | /ˈdʊrɔtɑr/ |

**Analysis: *-an* = personal name marker; *-ar* = toponymic marker**

The /n/ ↔ /r/ alternation is typologically well-motivated. Nasal-liquid alternation is attested across many language families, and the semantic extension from person → place (nominative → locative) is natural. Under this analysis:
- *Durotan* = *Duro-* + *-tan* = "person [named] Duro"
- *Durotar* = *Duro-* + *-tar* = "land [of] Duro" → "Durotan's land"

If *Gul'dan* also contains *-dan* as a personal name suffix, the /t/ ↔ /d/ voicing alternation (*-tan* vs. *-dan*) could reflect voicing assimilation: *Gul'* ends in /l/ (voiced sonorant) triggering /t/ → /d/, while *Duro* ends in /o/ (vowel) retaining /t/.

The *-tar* element also appears in *Lok'tar*, where it could originally have meant "place of victory" → "victory" by metonymy, or *-tar* in *Lok'tar* and *Durotar* could be homophonous but different morphemes.

**Confidence: Low–Moderate** for *-an/-tan* as a personal name suffix and *-ar/-tar* as a toponymic suffix. The Durotan/Durotar pair is the strongest evidence; the /n/ ↔ /r/ alternation (person ↔ place) is typologically motivated and phonologically clean.

### The *gora / ogar* Metathesis Observation

Two of the most culturally significant Orcish words share a striking phonological relationship:

| Word | IPA | Meaning | Consonant Skeleton |
|------|-----|---------|-------------------|
| gora | /ˈgɔrɑ/ | Honor | g-r |
| ogar | /ˈɔgɑr/ | Death | g-r |

Both words share the consonantal skeleton **/g...r/** and the vowel /ɑ/. If they are morphologically related, the /gɔrɑ/ ↔ /ɔgɑr/ pattern could reflect consonant-vowel metathesis of an ancient root *\*gVr* producing antonyms through rearrangement. This is speculative but culturally resonant: "honor" and "death" are thematically paired in orcish culture (cf. *Lok'tar ogar* = "Victory or death").

If *gora* and *ogar* share a root, then neither *-ra* nor *-ar* is a productive suffix in these words — both are just the vowel-consonant ending of a root that undergoes metathesis. This would argue against *-ar* as a general suffix (see Analysis B in the *-ar* pattern above).

**Confidence: Very Low–Low** for genuine metathesis. The pattern is striking but could reflect coincidence: /g/, /r/, /ɑ/, and /ɔ/ are all among the most common Orcish phonemes. The thematic pairing (honor/death) may be an artifact of the limited vocabulary rather than genuine morphological relationship.

### The *Grom-* Root: Productivity in Personal Names and Compounds

The element *Grom* /grɔm/ (inferred "giant's heart, bravery") appears in two attested words:

| Word | IPA | Context | Decomposition |
|------|-----|---------|---------------|
| Grommash | /ˈgrɔmɑʃ/ | Warchief of the Warsong clan | *Gromm* + *-ash* (or *Grom* + *-mash*) |
| Grombolar | /grɔmˈbɔlɑr/ | Uncertain meaning; possibly "blood and thunder" | *Grom* + *-bolar* |

If *Grom* = "bravery/giant's heart" (inferred from *Grommash* = "Heart of giant"), then:

- *Grommash* = *Grom* + *-mash* (where *-mash* may be an intensive or name-forming suffix, or simply *Gromm* with geminated /m/ + *-ash*)
- *Grombolar* = *Grom* + *-bolar* — if the folk etymology "blood and thunder" has any basis, *-bolar* might mean "thunder" or "blood-thunder"; alternatively, *-bolar* may be an unanalyzed element

The *Grom-* root demonstrates the same compounding pattern as *Lok'* (victory): both appear as the first element in multiple compounds, confirming that Orcish personal names are built from a limited set of productive roots combined with suffixes or second elements.

**Confidence: Low–Moderate** for *Grom* as a productive root. Two attestations establish a pattern, but *Grombolar*'s uncertain meaning prevents confident decomposition.

### The *-mar* Toponymic Suffix Hypothesis

The city name *Orgrimmar* /ˈɔrgrɪmɑr/ can be decomposed in multiple ways:

| Decomposition | Analysis | Notes |
|---------------|----------|-------|
| *Orgrim* + *-mar* | Personal name + toponymic suffix | Parallels *Durotan* → *Durotar*: founder's name + place suffix |
| *Org* + *-rimmar* | Unknown root + suffix | *-rimmar* has no other attestation |
| *Orgr* + *-immar* | Unknown root + suffix | *-immar* has no other attestation |

If *Orgrimmar* = *Orgrim* (the orcish leader Orgrim Doomhammer) + *-mar* (place suffix), then *-mar* /mɑr/ would be a second toponymic suffix alongside *-tar*:

| Suffix | Meaning | Evidence |
|--------|---------|----------|
| *-tar* /tɑr/ | Land/place (after personal name) | *Durotar* = "Duro's land" |
| *-mar* /mɑr/ | City/stronghold (after personal name) | *Orgrimmar* = "Orgrim's city" |

The semantic distinction between *-tar* (land/region) and *-mar* (city/fortress) would be typologically natural — many languages distinguish settlement nouns from regional nouns (cf. English *-ton* vs. *-land*, or German *-burg* vs. *-gau*).

**Alternative analysis**: *-mar* could be the same suffix as *-tar*, with /m/ ← /t/ a phonological development after /m/-final stems. If *Orgrim* ends in /m/, assimilation *-t-* → *-m-* would produce *-mar* from *-tar*. This would be a single toponymic suffix with phonologically conditioned allomorphy: *-tar* after non-labial consonants, *-mar* after labial consonants.

**Confidence: Very Low–Low** for *-mar* as an independent toponymic suffix. Only one attestation exists, and the assimilation analysis is equally plausible. If additional *-mar* place names are found (e.g., a hypothetical *\*[Name]mar* city), the suffix hypothesis would strengthen.

### The *-rand* Toponymic Suffix Hypothesis

The Draenor place name *Nagrand* /ˈnɑgrɑnd/ may decompose as *Nag* + *-rand*:

| Element | Possible Meaning | Notes |
|---------|-----------------|-------|
| *Nag* | "wind" or "spirit" (uncertain) | Folk etymology: "land of winds" or "land of spirits" |
| *-rand* | "land/region/edge" | cf. Dutch/German *rand* = "edge/border"; South African *rand* = "ridge" |

If *-rand* /rɑnd/ is a toponymic suffix meaning "land/region," it would join *-tar* and possibly *-mar* as Orcish place-name suffixes:

| Suffix | Meaning | Example |
|--------|---------|---------|
| *-tar* | Land/place | *Durotar* |
| *-mar* | City/stronghold (uncertain) | *Orgrimmar* |
| *-rand* | Land/region/borderland (uncertain) | *Nagrand* |

However, *Nagrand* may also be a single unanalyzed proper name, or *-rand* could be coincidental with the Germanic word. The place is on Draenor, a different planet from Azeroth, so real-world etymological parallels are design artifacts rather than in-universe cognates.

**Confidence: Very Low** for *-rand* as a genuine Orcish morpheme. Single attestation with no internal evidence beyond the folk etymology.

### The *-grim* Element and Cross-Language Design Convergence

The element *-grim* appears in two Orcish names:

| Name | Context | Possible Decomposition |
|------|---------|----------------------|
| Orgrim | Warchief, founder of the modern Horde | *Org* + *-grim* or single element |
| Orgrimmar | Capital city named after Orgrim | *Orgrim* + *-mar* (see above) |

In Vrykul, *-grim* /ɡrɪm/ means "mask/helmet/fierce" (from ON *gríma*, mask/helmet), as in *Bjarngrim* = "Bear-helm." If Orcish *-grim* carries a similar meaning ("fierce/ helmet"), then *Orgrim* = *Org* + *-grim* = "[Org]-fierce" or "[Org]-helm."

However, Orcish and Vrykul are from different planets (Draenor vs. Azeroth) with no genetic relationship. Any shared *-grim* morphology would be due to **Blizzard design convergence** — the developers used the same Norse-inspired element for both languages — rather than in-universe borrowing or inheritance. This is a confound for all cross-planet morphological comparisons in WoW.

**Confidence: Very Low** for Orcish *-grim* as a genuine morpheme. Even if *-grim* is analyzable in *Orgrim*, it cannot be distinguished from the name simply being monomorphemic. The Vrykul parallel is a design artifact.

## Syntax

### Word Order

No full sentences are attested, making syntactic analysis impossible. All surviving examples are:

- **Interjections / formulae:** *Zug zug*, *Lok'tar ogar*, *Throm-ka*
- **Compound nouns:** *Mak'gora*, *Kosh'harg*

If *Lok'tar ogar* reflects underlying syntax, the order is **V V** (victory death), but this is more likely a formulaic pair than evidence of verb-final or verb-initial order.

### Pragmatics

Orcish expressions serve specific pragmatic functions tied to orcish warrior culture:

| Function | Expression | Context |
|----------|------------|---------|
| Agreement / affirmation | *Zug zug*, *Kek* | Casual speech |
| Greeting | *Throm-ka* | Meeting |
| Battle cry | *Lok'tar*, *Lok'tar ogar* | Combat |
| Blessing | *Aka'Magosh* | Formal / ritual |
| Challenge | *Mak'gora* | Duel declaration |

The language appears to favor **fixed formulae** over generative syntax for common social functions, consistent with a tradition-oriented warrior culture.

Notes:
- /θ/ (spelled "th") appears in *Throm*, *Thrall* — a dental fricative, likely an allophone of /t/ in specific environments or a marginal phoneme.
- /dʒ/ (j) is uncertain; no clear attestation in the limited vocabulary. Its inclusion is provisional based on the /ʃ/ phoneme suggesting the postalveolar series may be complete.

### Consonant Clusters

Orcish permits heavy onset and coda clusters:

- **Onset clusters**: /gr/ (*Grom*), /thr/ (*Throm*, *Thrall*), /gl/ (*Gul'dan*), /sk/ (inferred)
- **Coda clusters**: /ʃ/ (*Grommash*, *Garrosh*), /rg/ (*harg*), /kt/ (inferred from stop-heavy pattern)
- **Medial clusters**: /mm/ (*Grommash*), /rr/ (*Garrosh*) — gemination or double consonants

Notes:
- /a/ is the most frequent vowel, appearing in nearly every attested word.

### Phonological Patterns

1. **Guttural preference**: A preponderance of velar and glottal consonants (/g/, /k/, /h/) gives Orcish its characteristic harsh, guttural quality. This is the most distinctive phonological feature.

2. **Consonant-heavy syllables**: Most attested words are CVC or CVC.CVC structures. Open syllables (CV) are less common. The language favors closed, heavy syllables — *Lok*, *gora*, *harg*, *zug*, *kosh*.

3. **Prominent /r/**: The alveolar trill /r/ appears frequently, often in clusters (*Grom*, *Throm*, *Garrosh*, *Saurfang*). It is one of the most salient consonants in the language's sound profile.

4. **Stress pattern**: Cannot be determined from written attestations alone. Based on the typical WoW voice acting for orcish names, primary stress may fall on the first syllable (*LOK-tar*, *MAK-go-ra*), but this is [speculative].

5. **Gemination**: Doubled consonants appear in *Grommash* (/m:/), *Garrosh* (/r:/). Whether this is phonemic gemination or simply orthographic doubling is unclear.

6. **Apostrophe as possible glottal stop**: Unlike Darnassian, where apostrophes mark purely orthographic morpheme boundaries, the Orcish apostrophe (*Lok'tar*, *Mak'gora*) may correspond to a glottal stop /ʔ/ in speech — the percussive quality of orcish pronunciation would be consistent with this. However, this is [speculative].

7. **Sound symbolism**: The harsh, percussive phonology aligns with orcish cultural values — strength, directness, combat readiness. The prevalence of stops and gutturals over fricatives and approximants reflects this aesthetic. Compare Darnassian, which favors liquids and sibilants, producing a melodic quality matching night elf culture.

8. **Back-vowel harmony tendency**: Many Orcish morphemes show co-occurring /a/ and /o/: *Lok'tar* (o-a), *ogar* (o-a), *Mak'gora* (a-o-a), *Kosh'harg* (o-a), *Throm-ka* (o-a), *Aka'Magosh* (a-a-o), *Grommash* (o-a). Both /o/ and /a/ are back vowels, and their co-occurrence within morphemes is far more frequent than /o/-/i/ or /a/-/u/ mixing. Front vowels (/i/, /e/) are rare and tend to appear in isolation (*Kek*, e-only). This suggests a weak back-vowel harmony domain where /a/ and /o/ are the dominant vowels.

9. **Pre-apostrophe phonotactic permissiveness**: Unlike the elven languages (Darnassian, Thalassian), which show a near-categorical constraint against oral stops before the apostrophe (~97% sonorant/fricative/vowel only), Orcish freely permits oral stops: *Lok'* /k/, *Mak'* /k/, *Gol'* /l/. This mirrors Zandali's similar permissiveness (*Lok'*, *Mak'*, *Zeb'*, *Krag'*) and contrasts with the elven innovation, suggesting that the pre-apostrophe sonorant constraint is an areal feature limited to the elven branch.

## Summary of Limitations

- No full sentences are attested; syntax is entirely unknown.
- Morphemic analysis of compounds rests on very few data points and is circular.
- The cross-faction language filter generates many "Orcish" strings that are **not** genuine vocabulary.
- Blizzard has not published any official grammar or lexicon.
- All rules above are **hypotheses**, not confirmed features of the language.
